# CAP 562 Notes: Chunk 11 (pages 1138 to 1257)

All of this chunk is ATA Chapter 51 (Standard Practices and Structures), CAP 562 Book 2. Page dates are mostly 15 April 2011, with some pages dated 31 October 2012 (51-90 p4, 51-100 p1 and p9) and 30 November 2011 (51-170 Appendix 1). There is little regulatory or BCAR content. The chunk is almost entirely maintenance-practice guidance. Leaflets 51-70 and 51-80 are the main exceptions: they cover what triggers inspections and how life-limited components are tracked.

## 2. BCAR and National Regime

This chunk contains no direct explanation of Certificates of Airworthiness, Permits to Fly, LAA or BMAA, Annex I or ex-military aircraft. The BCAR references it does contain are:

- **BCAR Chapter A6-7 (UK-manufactured aircraft) and B6-7 (foreign-manufactured aircraft)**
  - Applies to structural repairs that are "of a major nature or not covered in the particular approved manual".
  - The approved organisation, or the appropriately licensed aircraft maintenance engineer, should tell the nearest CAA area office about the repair **before work commences** (51-90 para 1.3).
  - A6-7 and B6-7 also give the CAA's requirements for certification after repair (51-90 para 8).
- **BCAR Chapter A2-5 / B2-5**: repair schemes the CAA has not previously approved are normally investigated as **modifications** under these procedures.
- **BCAR Section A Chapter A6-2 / Section B Chapter B6-2**: disturbed parts of aircraft or engine control systems must be **inspected in duplicate before they are concealed** (51-90 para 3.11.2 note).
- **BCAR Chapter A8-10**: "welders must be approved by the CAA" (51-90 para 6.1.1). This is the CAA welder approval scheme.
- **BCAR Chapter D4-1** (with EASA CS 25.607): an approved means of locking must be provided on all connecting elements in primary structure, fluid systems, controls and other mechanical systems essential to safe operation (51-100 para 1). Section D is the large-aeroplane design code.
- **Licence limitation** (51-90 para 4.2 note): complete detail parts must be made by suitably Approved Organisations to the appropriate drawings. "The holder of an Aircraft Maintenance Engineer's Licence in Category B is not authorised to certify the manufacture of aircraft parts." This refers to the old BCAR AMEL categories.
- **Design approval source**: if damage is not covered by the Repair Manual, a repair scheme can be obtained from the aircraft manufacturer "or to a Drawing Office holding the appropriate Design Approval" (51-90 para 2.1.1). This points to BCAR A8-1-style design organisations (my inference; the chunk does not name A8-1).
- **Repair data acceptability**: repairs must follow the Repair Manual or approved repair drawings, together with other documents "recognised or approved by the CAA/EASA". For American general aviation aircraft without specific repair data, **FAA AC 43.13-1B Change 1** may be used for guidance.
- **Certificate of fitness for flight**: after some repairs a "certificate of fitness for flight should be issued and the aircraft should be tested in flight" (51-90 para 8.2). This is the old BCAR mechanism for a check or test flight.
- **Fabric check before C of A renewal**: on light aircraft, fabric should be checked at Maintenance Schedule periods "and prior to renewal of the Certificate of Airworthiness" (51-150 para 11). This reflects the old renewable national C of A.
- **CAP 747 Generic Requirements** cited:
  - GR No. 16: nitrogen tyre inflation, formerly Airworthiness Notice No. 70.
  - GR No. 10: Painting of Aircraft.
  - GR No. 23: NDT personnel qualification.
- **Fatigue ADs**: all Additional Airworthiness Directives on fatigue that the CAA issued for foreign aircraft types have been **removed from CAP 747**. Fatigue and ageing aircraft are being handled by EASA's Ageing Aircraft Rulemaking Working Group **MDM.028** (51-80 para 1).
- **Laboratory acceptance**: fabric bursting-strength samples go to "a laboratory acceptable to the CAA" (51-150 para 11.4).
- **Fire-damage samples**: mechanical testing is done by "an approved test house" (51-90 para 3.9.1).

## 3. Obligations and Numbers

### 51-70 Abnormal occurrences
- **Heavy landing**
  - Consult the flight crew about weight, fuel distribution, landing conditions and any noises.
  - Some aircraft have heavy landing indicators.
  - The inspection covers primary areas (landing gear, its supporting structure, wing and tailplane attachments, engine mountings) and secondary areas (fuselage and wing skin and structure).
  - On some types, secondary areas need not be inspected if the primary areas are clear. If damage is found in a primary area, the inspection must continue.
  - Checklists cover landing gear (8 items, including jacking and retraction and steering tests), mainplanes (6), fuselage (9, including inertia switches and gyro erection time), engines (8, including propeller shaft shock-loading check and chip detectors) and tail unit (3).
  - Engine runs are done only if no major structural distortion has been found. On turbines, check run-down time.
  - If damage is found: carry out rigging and symmetry checks (51-120), check cable tensions where controls pass through structure, and do a cabin leak rate check on pressurised aircraft.
- **Burst tyre**
  - The burst wheel must normally be returned for overhaul.
  - On multi-wheel gear, all tyres on that leg or axle may need to be discarded or removed for examination.
  - On wheels without fusible plugs, check for a binding brake.
  - Some highly stressed bolts may need NDT.
  - Check rear-mounted engines for ingested debris.
- **Tyre inflation (GR 16, formerly Airworthiness Notice No. 70)**
  - Applies to all braked wheels of retractable landing gear on aeroplanes **exceeding 5700 kg**.
  - Inflate with nitrogen or another inert gas, with oxygen **not greater than 5% by volume**.
  - Where no inert gas is available, air may be used if an **entry is made in the Technical Log**. The tyre must then be reinflated or serviced to the agreed procedure "at the earliest opportunity or within **25 flight hours**, whichever is the sooner".
- **Severe turbulence**
  - Accelerometer or fatigue meter readings beyond **-0.5 g and +2.5 g** on transport aircraft suggest damage is possible. Meter peaks may exaggerate the real loads.
  - On aerobatic or semi-aerobatic types (meters seldom fitted), any reported turbulence should always be investigated.
  - Carry out the paragraph 2 inspections, usually without the landing gear items.
- **Lightning**
  - Strike damage is found at wing tips, leading edges, the tail and the nose. Static discharge damage is found at tips, trailing edges and antennae.
  - Check bonding strips, static wicks and control surface hinge bearings.
  - Some manufacturers require oil filters and chip detectors to be examined, and this check repeated "for a specified number of running hours after the occurrence".
  - If the gear was down, check for residual magnetism and demagnetise.
  - Carry out functional checks of radio, radar, instruments, compasses, electrics and flying controls. Some types need a radome bonding resistance check.
  - From 51-90: compasses should be check swung, and magnetised steel parts demagnetised (Leaflet F-70).
- **Jet blast**: check control surfaces driven against their stops, dents, windscreens and intakes. Light aircraft need more inspection if they were moved.
- **Helicopters**
  - Rear fuselage and tail boom: check for blade strike.
  - Remove the main rotor blades and check for twist.
  - If the blades are badly damaged, the main rotor shaft, pitch change rods and main gearbox mounting bolts may need removal.
  - Check the rotor head hinges and dampers.
  - Tail rotor damage beyond limits means inspection or replacement of the hub, links, gearbox and driveshaft.
  - Skid gear: check cross-tube bow. Floats: check for leaks and strap condition.
- **Rotor overspeed**
  - Below a specified limit: check the blades and head.
  - Above the limit: remove the main and tail rotor heads for overhaul.
  - If the blades are damaged as well: remove the head, shaft, pitch rods, tail rotor and transmission for overhaul.
- **Other occurrences**, for example flap limiting speed exceeded: inspect the flaps, hinge brackets and mechanism. If satisfactory, the aircraft may be considered airworthy.

### 51-80 Fatigue lives
- Major components such as wings and centre-sections on some types have lives limited by **flying hours, flights or landings**. This now applies to some smaller private aircraft as well as older large transports.
- Compliance with the manufacturer's ICA and Service Bulletins on fatigue lives "is required by the CAA and EASA".
- Life limits assume likely average utilisation. Atypical operations need the limits reassessed, which may change the Safe Fatigue Lives. Examples are low-level maritime surveillance, geological survey (especially in pressurised aircraft) and long-endurance work.
- **Definitions**
  - **Landing**: "the main undercarriage wheels make contact with the airfield surface and lift is significantly destroyed".
  - **Flight**: one flight goes with each landing, so total flights (pressurised plus unpressurised) equal total landings.
  - **Pressurised flight**: pressurisation operated at a differential of **14 kN/m2 (2 lb/in2) or above**.
- When a lifed component is **installed**, a record must be kept with the aircraft documents of the hours flown and flights or landings it has already done. The pressure differential may also matter.
- **A Certificate of Release to Service may not be signed until the signatory is satisfied the component's history has been established.**
- If there is no such evidence, owners or operators may be required to replace the component early.
- The CAA requires records of **operating hours and cycles** for most engines, APUs and propellers. Cycles are defined by each manufacturer.

### 51-90 Repair of metal airframes
- Major repairs, or repairs not covered by the approved manual: notify the CAA area office before starting (BCAR A6-7 / B6-7).
- Where possible, survey the damage on site before moving the aircraft. Plot the distortion on a station chart and photograph it.
- While the aircraft is supported for repair, a responsible person should check **daily** that it has not moved. This ranges from checking jack seals are unbroken to theodolite checks.
- If the fuel has not been drained, verify the maximum permissible jacking weight before lifting.
- Trestle formers should be lined with dry felt and covered with polythene. Do not use rubber.
- **Dent acceptance example**: a smooth round dent can be negligible if its depth is within Repair Data limits and its smallest linear dimension is **at least 15 times its depth**. For example, 1.2 mm deep needs at least 18 mm across.
- Holes elongated by **stress**: the part must be renewed. Elongation from **wear** beyond limits is handled by rectification schemes such as oversize bushes, reaming or oversize bolts.
- Bolts whose nuts were locked by peening must not be re-used.
- After repairs in pressurised areas or fuel tanks, a leak test or pressure test may be specified.
- Fire damage
  - An eddy current conductivity test may show the extent (Leaflet F-80). If in doubt, cut out samples for an approved test house.
  - Renew magnesium, plastics and rubber parts that were near a fire, including bag tanks against an affected tank bay wall.
- Highly stressed parts repaired by welding need NDT afterwards.
- Inner sleeve tube splice: diagonal cuts at **30° or 45°**, with a weld gap of about **2.4 mm (3/32 in)**. Outer sleeves are used only where inner sleeves are impracticable.
- Gauging blend depth: depth = D minus (A+B)/2. The adaptor block edge has a radius of about 1.2 mm. Measure before applying protective treatment.
- **Bow limit**: bow = clearance divided by length. "In general a maximum bow of **1 in 600** is normally acceptable unless otherwise stated in the Repair Manual." For example, 0.040 in over 24 in gives 1/600.
- Repairs to control surfaces need a balance check. Repairs to them are often limited in area and position.
- After repair, depending on its nature:
  - Enter the full details in the log book.
  - Sign a CRS.
  - Weigh the aircraft and amend or replace the Weight and CG Schedule.
  - Issue a certificate of fitness for flight.
  - Carry out a flight test, with particulars and results provided.

### 51-100 Locking devices
- Split pins are corrosion-resisting steel. Table 1 gives sizes for bolts from 1/4 in to 1 in, under British SP 90 and American MS 24665. These differ: for example, a 3/8 in bolt takes a 1/16 in SP 90 pin or a 3/32 in MS pin. Check the hole size before fitting.
- **Single use**: split pins, shakeproof washers, locking wire, and fibre or nylon friction-element fasteners.
- Tab washer tabs must not be bent more than once. A multiple-tab washer can be re-used after removing the used tab and inspecting the rest.
- Do not enlarge split-pin holes or file pins or nuts. Never ease a nut back to line up a pin. Where torque is not specified, a slight further tightening is allowed.
- Renew spring washers at overhaul or repair. This is "essential" in engines and in units with reciprocating parts.
- Locking plates can be re-used while they still fit the hexagon well.
- Obtain circlips and locking rings **by part number**, not by comparing them with the old item. Some are single use, and some have limits on the gap or its radial position.
- **Wire locking**
  - Material is corrosion-resisting steel or heat-resisting nickel alloy.
  - Final twist: **at least 13 mm (1/2 in)**.
  - Untwisted length: **no more than 9.5 mm (3/8 in)**.
  - Unsupported length: normally **no more than 76 mm (3 in)**.
  - Approach angle: **at least 45°** to the rotational axis, with a tangential approach.
  - The lay of the wire must resist loosening, so check for left or right hand threads.
  - Emergency controls and switches are locked with thin **copper wire** ("wire sealing").
- **Stiffnuts**
  - The male thread must protrude at least **one full thread** (not counting the chamfer) through the friction element.
  - All-metal stiffnuts must not be re-used in locations vital to safety, such as control runs.
  - Manufacturers usually require in-built torque to be checked with a torque wrench.
  - Finger test for small non-vital nuts: if the nut can be turned far enough by hand for the thread to protrude through the friction element, it is rejected.
- Peening is done only when specified. About **1.5 threads** should protrude.
- Taper pins have a taper of **1 in 48**.
- Loctite has **DTD 900 Approval No. 4588**. On non-degreased parts, locking strength drops by about **15%**. Use it only when approved data specifies it.
- When using Araldite, mix a separate sample to check it hardens within the specified time.

### 51-110 Critical bolted joints
- A "critical bolted joint" is one with high stress where poor assembly could cause fatigue failure. Examples are spar joints, tailplane and wing attachments, and engine mountings.
- Check each hole with its own assembly bolt.
- With multiple bolts, tighten progressively, finger-tight first. New parts may need bedding in: tighten to half the pre-load, slacken, then do the final tightening.
- Jointing compound
  - DTD 369 is not suitable above **200°C**.
  - Hard-setting compounds are unsuitable where there is vibration.
  - Tighten the joint while the compound is still wet.
  - Sealant not used within its application time must be discarded.
- PLI washers can be used only once and only with self-locking nuts.
- Standard spanners (BS 192) are seldom acceptable as the only tightening method. In an emergency, torque can be applied with a spring balance pulled at 90° to the spanner.
- **Bolts should be extracted for detailed examination at the periods specified in the approved Maintenance Schedule.** Some aircraft need bolts removed from critical joints more often than at major overhaul.
  - Before removing bolts, support the structure so the joint carries no load.
  - Examine the bolts for steps, cracks, fretting and corrosion. Use NDT, normally magnetic (F-70) for steel bolts.
  - Replate or discard bolts with damaged plating. If in doubt, reject the bolt.
  - Examine the holes before and after cleaning (for example with trichloroethylene), using an endoscope or eddy current.
  - Fretting shows as black or grey dust in aluminium or brown rust in steel. Inspect the flat faying surfaces too, using dye penetrant, eddy current or ultrasonic surface wave methods.

### 51-120 Rigging
- Do rigging checks indoors where possible. If outside, position the aircraft nose into wind and do not lift in strong winds or gusts.
- Weight and loading must match the manual or the original rigging chart. Do not exceed the maximum jacking weight.
- Brakes should be off and wheels chocked, except on levered-suspension undercarriage, where the wheels are left unchocked.
- Apply hydraulic jack locks as soon as the aircraft is positioned.
- Dihedral and incidence should be checked **at specified periods and after heavy landings or abnormal flight loads**. Nominal figures and tolerances are in the manual, but **the actual figures for the individual aircraft are recorded in the aircraft log book**.
- Check sequence: wing dihedral, wing incidence (at least two stations, inboard and outboard), engine alignment, tailplane level or dihedral, tailplane incidence, fin verticality, symmetry.
- For symmetry checks with a tape, use about **5 lb** tension with a spring balance on long distances.
- Biplanes
  - Screw fork-ends the same number of turns at each end.
  - Re-rig to the log book figures if using the same components, since these may correct a known flying fault.
  - Set adjustable tailplanes to neutral before checking.
- Sighting rod readings must be within tolerance and **entered in the aircraft log book for permanent record**. Graduated rods are marked in **1/4°** steps. After a component change (for example a wing or tailplane), repeat the initial check.

### 51-130 Disturbed airflow
- The laminar to turbulent transition point may be designed to be at **30 to 50% of chord**.
- If handling faults are reported, use the full range of ground-adjustable trim before searching for profile defects.
- Leading edge paint thickness affects trim. Restored leading edge contours should be checked with a contour jig.
- After changing a control surface or aerofoil part, check aerodynamic alignment.

### 51-140 Paint
- Stoving temperature is normally **below 125°C (257°F)**.
- **Paint shop conditions**
  - Temperature **15 to 25°C (60 to 77°F)**, humidity **below 75%**.
  - DTD 5555 epoxies must be applied **above 18°C (65°F)**.
  - Etch primers need humidity **of at least 30%**.
  - Air change every **2 minutes**, with the shop at slight positive pressure.
  - Remove dried paint and spray dust **at least once a week**.
- Spray gun air pressure **2.75 to 5.5 bar (40 to 80 lbf/in2)**. Pressure-feed pot **0.3 to 1.03 bar (5 to 15 lbf/in2)**. Hold the gun **15 to 25 cm (6 to 10 in)** from the surface.
- Strain paint through metal gauze of **60 to 120 mesh**.
- Drain the compressor tank **daily**, and keep a record of equipment servicing.
- Each operation (pre-treatment, cleaning, painting, and repairs and inspections done earlier) must be checked and **certified by an inspector**, because later coats hide earlier work.
- Do not mix paints from different manufacturers unless they are proved compatible.
- For paint repairs, the filler must be the same material as the top coat.
- Mark paint containers with the date of receipt, use them in rotation, and do not hold them beyond their storage life.
- Comply with HSE COSHH.

### 51-150 Fabric
- Covering conditions: **16 to 21°C**, relative humidity **no more than 70%**.
- Materials
  - Linen to BS F1 (unsupported covers). Cotton to BS F8, F57, F116 or DTD 575A.
  - Madapolam BS F114 for wooden surfaces.
  - Egyptian tape BS F47.
  - Thread BS F34: No. 40 (3 kg), No. 18 (7.25 kg) or No. 30 (4.5 kg).
  - Cord BS F35 flax, or DTD 5620 nylon.
  - Store at about 20°C.
- Follow the original manufacturer's covering method unless an alternative is approved.
- Seams
  - Balloon seam: 16 mm fold, 4 stitches/cm, 2 lines 9 mm apart.
  - Lap seam (only if specified): 31 mm overlap, with a 75 mm tape over it.
  - Never make seams at right angles to the airflow, except at leading and trailing edges.
- Overhand stitch: 3 per cm, with a lock stitch about every 50 mm.
- **Stringing**
  - Pitch is normally **75 mm (3 in)**. Use **37 mm (1.5 in)** in the slipstream area or on aircraft **over 910 kg (2000 lb)**.
  - Use a double knot at the first and last loops and every **450 mm (18 in)**.
  - Cover with 37 mm tape.
  - The slipstream area is the propeller diameter plus one rib each side. On multi-engined aircraft, the whole gap between slipstreams also counts.
  - Boom stringing is used on deep sections. Alternate rib and boom stringing is used on sections 150 to 300 mm deep.
- Inspectors must make sure **drain holes are clear**. Shielded eyelets go only in specified positions, with the shroud facing the correct way.
- **Repairs**
  - Herring-bone stitch: at least 2 per cm, 6 mm from the edge, lock knot every 150 mm, 25 mm tape, patch overlapping 37 mm.
  - Woods frame repair: up to **50 mm square**, at least 50 mm clear of seams. The frame is 0.8 mm cellulose, at least 25 mm wide, with 12 mm corner radii.
  - Darning: hole no more than 50 mm wide, 37 mm patch overlap.
  - Insertion repair: damage **over 100 mm square**. Patch 25 mm larger, edges folded under 12 mm. Above 225 mm square, a 75 mm tape is often used.
  - Boot stitch: No. 18 BS F34 thread, lock knot every 150 mm.
- **Fabric strength**
  - Light aircraft: check at Maintenance Schedule periods **and before renewal of the C of A**.
  - Large aircraft fabric may have an arbitrary life.
  - Fabric with **at least 70% of new-fabric strength** may be considered airworthy. Fabric only just within the range should be checked more often.
  - A portable penetrating-cone tester is suitable only where the dope has penetrated the fabric. Do not test through a double layer of fabric. Take the lowest reading as representative.
  - Repair test punctures with a **50 or 75 mm** patch.
  - Butyrate (CAB) or non-penetrating dope needs a **laboratory test**: bursting strength, samples about 87 mm in diameter, at a CAA-acceptable lab (FED-STD-191 Methods 5120 and 5122).
  - New-fabric tensile tests use six warp and six weft samples, 62 by 300 to 400 mm.
- Polyester: shrink with a household iron at about **120°C**. Glass fibre: 50 mm doped seams and 50 mm patch overlap. Some glass-fibre fabric is CAB-only.

### 51-160 Doping
- Old dope becomes acidic and rots fabric. Use fresh dope only.
- Retarder may be used at up to **1 part retarder to 4 parts thinners**. Butyrate gloss can be improved with up to **20% retarder** in the last coat.
- Shop conditions: air **21 to 26°C**, ideal humidity **45 to 50%**, workable range **20 to 70%**. Table 1 gives relative humidity from dry bulb and wet bulb depression. For example, 17°C with a 3°C depression gives 72%.
- **Safety**
  - Ground the structure being doped, and for nitrate dope ground the gun and the operator too.
  - Wet floors before sweeping.
  - Mixed dope and zinc chromate overspray can cause spontaneous combustion.
  - Use explosion-proof lamps and a floor-level fan. Do not mix with drill-driven stirrers.
- **BS X26 schemes**: 751 is low tautness (light structures), **752 is the standard medium tautness scheme**, and 753 is high tautness. Tables give coat weights in g/m2 and the number of coats. Weight tolerance is **±20%**.
- Natural fabric
  - First coat: nitrate, thinned 25 to 50%, brushed in, with fungicide.
  - On wings, dope aft of the front spar first.
  - Tapes go on after **at least 1 hour** of drying.
  - Eyelets may be soaked in thinners for **no more than 2 minutes**.
  - Aluminium coat must be lightproof, checked with a light inside.
  - At least **three** pigmented finish coats.
  - Wait **at least a month** before polishing, and wax **at least once a year**.
- Polyester: first coat nitrate, 2 to 3 parts dope to 1 part thinners, no fungicide, 400 grit sanding.
- Glass fibre: **nitrate dope must never be used**. Butyrate only, up to five coats to fill.
- **Dope weight check**
  - Dope a test panel at the same time and weigh it before and after.
  - US Military Specifications call for a minimum of **161 g/m2 (4.75 oz/yd2)**.
- **After re-covering or re-doping, the aircraft must be re-weighed and a new Weight Schedule raised (Leaflet 8-10).**
- After re-covering or doping control surfaces, check **static balance** against the manufacturer's requirements, because there is a flutter risk.

### 51-170 Composites
- The leaflet applies where there are "no, or incomplete, manufacturer instruction[s]".
- Examples given: Cessna C172 tip fairings, Europa and gliders (primary structure), and T67 and Grob 115 (complete airframes).
- When there is unscheduled access to normally inaccessible structure, inspect everything exposed. Operators should add such opportunities to the Maintenance Schedule.
- **Cleaning**
  - Hand sand loose paint with grade 320 paper, not beyond the primer.
  - Do not use blasting (including plastic media) or chemical strippers such as methylene chloride unless the manufacturer permits.
  - Clean with MEK or IPA.
  - Do not use high pressure hot water or steam.
  - Dry at up to **50°C** for elevated-temperature-cured materials, or a maximum of **30°C** for room-temperature-cured materials.
  - Check drain paths afterwards.
- **Inspection**
  - About **90%** of composite inspections are visual, often with a tap test.
  - Visual inspection at **no more than 1.52 m (5 ft)**. Detailed visual inspection at about **0.2 to 0.3 m (8 to 12 in)**, with a 10X magnifier.
  - Impact dents may recover **60% or more** of the original profile, so the time between the event and the inspection matters.
  - Light test works for GFRP only, not CFRP. Grob and Slingsby recommend it.
  - Tap test detects defects down to about **12.7 to 25.7 mm** diameter, exploring in **6 to 13 mm** steps.
  - NDT must be done by qualified personnel (CAP 747 GR No. 23).
- **Damage**
  - About **80%** of damage is impact, often from ground handling.
  - Classify structure as **Primary** or Secondary. If in doubt, treat it as Primary. Components may be zoned.
  - Classify damage as **cosmetic** (dry and seal it if fluid could get in) or **structural**: penetration, inter-ply (delamination, disbond) or intra-ply (fibre, matrix, heat, lightning, fluid ingress, matrix cracking, porosity, fibre-matrix disbond), plus sandwich skin and core damage.
  - If in doubt, treat an outer ply as structural.
  - Fibre damage, especially to primary structure, must be repaired immediately. Non-cosmetic fatigue damage needs immediate repair.
- **Disbond failure modes**
  - **Cohesive** failure means an overload. Start a wider inspection, for example a heavy landing inspection, unless a local event explains it.
  - **Adhesive** failure means under-strength adhesive.
  - **Smooth interface** failure is potentially catastrophic contamination. Repair the whole joint unless it is shown to be at full strength.
- Lightning: trim an extra **25 to 55 mm** margin. Check electrical paths and static wicks, and use bond testers to check resistance.
- Hot and wet conditions reduce compressive strength by 10 to 15% (room temperature to 50°C, 0.6% moisture). Cold and dry conditions reduce tensile strength by 5 to 10% (to -56°C).
- Metallic structure usually shows fatigue only in the last **5%** of its life. Composites show damage throughout their life, with up to ten times the number of damage sites.
- **Repairs**
  - Areas marked non-repairable (in the Maintenance Manual or Flight Manual): consult the manufacturer immediately.
  - Otherwise use approved data, a controlled environment and qualified personnel.
  - Consider the effect on CG.
  - Control surfaces may need balancing.
  - Restore lightning protection.
- Any change of paint scheme must strictly follow the manufacturer's instructions (CAP 747 GR No. 10).
- **Appendix 1**
  - Dye penetrant is effectively destructive on composites.
  - Low frequency bond testers (below 100 kHz) need no couplant. High frequency testers (25 to 500 kHz) need couplant and work to 0.5 in thickness and 0.5 in defects.
  - Ultrasonic through-transmission needs access to both sides. Pulse echo works from one side. C-scan maps the part.
  - Radiography uses low voltage. Zinc iodide penetrant cannot be removed.
  - Also covered: mechanical impedance, thermography (moisture), and moisture meters (not usable on carbon or near metal).

## 4. Records, Forms and Documents

- **Technical Log entry**: records that a tyre was inflated with air where nitrogen was unavailable (GR 16), which starts a 25 flight hour or earliest-opportunity reinflation deadline.
- **Lifed component history record**: kept with the aircraft documents when a fatigue-lifed component is installed. Records hours flown and flights or landings already sustained, and the pressure differential where relevant. The CRS signatory must be satisfied the history is established before signing.
- **Engine, APU and propeller records**: operating hours and cycles, with cycles defined by the manufacturer.
- **Station chart and photographs** of damage and distortion, made before the aircraft is moved (51-90).
- **Repair report** (51-90 para 3.11)
  - Details all repair work and procedures.
  - Quotes approved repair scheme reference numbers.
  - Records any opportunity maintenance done during the repair, such as wear rectification.
- **Inspection Record Sheet**: lists the stage inspections in sequence with the repair report. Examples are rivet and bolt hole inspection, structure before closing (workmanship, protection, security, locking) and duplicate inspection of controls.
- **Duplicate inspection** of disturbed control systems before they are concealed (BCAR A6-2 / B6-2).
- **Log book entry** with full particulars of the repair, plus a **Certificate of Release to Service** (51-90 para 8.1).
- **Weight and Centre of Gravity Schedule**, amended or replaced after repair (51-90), or a **new Weight Schedule** after re-covering or re-doping (51-160, Leaflet 8-10).
- **Certificate of fitness for flight**, plus flight test particulars and results (51-90 para 8.2).
- **Aircraft log book rigging figures**: the actual dihedral, incidence, symmetry and wire-length figures for the individual aircraft, and sighting rod readings "for permanent record" (51-120).
- **Rigging chart or rigging diagram** from the manufacturer (nominal values and tolerances).
- **Paint shop records**: inspector certification of each pre-treatment and painting stage, a record of equipment servicing, and container receipt dates.
- **Fabric test results**: portable tester readings and lab bursting strength results, compared against the 70% threshold.
- **Test panel weights** for dope film weight.
- **Repair schemes and approved repair drawings**: from the Repair Manual, the manufacturer, or a Drawing Office with Design Approval. Unapproved schemes are treated as BCAR A2-5 / B2-5 modifications.

## 5. Practice Guidance (summary for maintenance and planning features)

- **Occurrence-driven inspections (51-70)**: event types are heavy or overweight landing, burst tyre, tyre explosion or fire, severe turbulence, lightning strike, jet blast, rotor overspeed (by degree), flap overspeed and other events. Each has a structured checklist, primary and secondary zones, and follow-ups (rigging check, cabin leak check, engine run, repeat chip detector checks for N hours, compass swing).
- **Lifed structure (51-80)**: track hours, flights, landings and pressurised flights per component. Transferred components must bring their history with them.
- **Repairs (51-90)**
  - Classify damage as negligible, repairable or replace.
  - Apply the dent ratio (15 times depth) and bow (1 in 600) default limits.
  - Renew parts with stress-elongated holes.
  - Renew magnesium, plastic and rubber parts after a fire.
  - Welding by CAA-approved welders, with NDT after.
  - Control surface rebalancing after repair.
  - Leak or pressure test after repairs to pressurised areas or fuel tanks.
- **Locking (51-100, 51-110)**: a reference list of device types with their re-use rules (single use or re-usable on condition) and wire-locking geometry limits. Critical joints need periodic bolt extraction per the Maintenance Schedule, with NDT of bolts and holes.
- **Rigging (51-120)**: rigging checks at scheduled periods, after abnormal loads and after component changes. Results are compared against the aircraft's own log book baseline.
- **Aerodynamic condition (51-130)**: inspection points are cowling gaps, door seals, flush caps, vortex generators, wing fences, shroud clearances and spoiler dwell. Seals should be checked after freezing wet conditions.
- **Paint and dope shop controls (51-140, 51-160)**: environmental limits on temperature and humidity, material pot life and shelf life, schemes and coat counts, and inspector stage certification.
- **Fabric (51-150)**: periodic strength testing with a 70% acceptance threshold and closer monitoring near the limit, repair size limits by method, and drain holes kept clear.
- **Composites (51-170)**: damage taxonomy, inspection distances, tap test, a wider inspection triggered by a cohesive disbond, immediate repair for fibre or structural damage, non-repairable zones, and control surface balance and CG after repair.

## 6. Software Implications

1. **Occurrence and Event object** (types: heavy_landing, overweight_landing, burst_tyre, tyre_explosion, severe_turbulence, lightning_strike, jet_blast, rotor_overspeed, flap_overspeed, composite_cohesive_disbond, other)
   - Fields: date, aircraft, reported g-meter reading, aircraft weight, fuel distribution, crew narrative, overspeed degree (for rotors).
   - Creates a task card from a checklist template for each event type, with primary and secondary zones and a "primary damage found, so continue to secondary" branch.
   - Follow-up tasks: rigging and symmetry check, cabin leak rate check, compass swing, radome bonding check, demagnetisation, repeat oil filter and chip detector checks for N running hours.
   - A turbulence event on a transport aircraft beyond -0.5 g or +2.5 g suggests damage is possible. Turbulence on aerobatic types should always be investigated.
2. **Tyre inflation gas tracking** (aeroplanes over 5700 kg with braked retractable gear)
   - Fields: gas type (N2 or air), oxygen content limit 5% by volume, Technical Log reference.
   - If air is used, set an alert for the earlier of the next opportunity or 25 FH.
3. **Life-limited structural component** record
   - Fields: life units (FH, flights, landings, pressurised flights at 2 psi or more), limits, utilisation profile flag (atypical operation means the limits need reassessment), history at installation (hours, flights, landings, pressure differential), source evidence, prior aircraft.
   - **Block CRS** if the history has not been established.
   - Fields for engine, APU and propeller cycles, with a reference to the manufacturer's cycle definition.
   - Counter rule: landings equal flights. Add a flag for pressurised flights.
4. **Repair record**
   - Fields: damage classification (negligible, repairable, replace), repair data source (Repair Manual, manufacturer scheme, Design-Approved Drawing Office, AC 43.13-1B for US GA, unapproved), "major or not in manual" flag that drives a **CAA notification before work** task (BCAR A6-7 / B6-7), modification linkage (A2-5 / B2-5), approved scheme reference numbers, photos and station chart, opportunity maintenance.
   - Linked Inspection Record Sheet (stage inspections, duplicate inspection items for control systems before closure).
   - Post-repair actions: log book entry, CRS, re-weigh and W&B update, control surface balance, leak or pressure test, NDT after welding, certificate of fitness for flight, flight test results.
   - Welder approval check: the welder's CAA approval (A8-10) must be valid.
   - Licence-scope rule: a Category B AMEL cannot certify the manufacture of parts.
5. **Damage measurement helpers**
   - Dent acceptance: smallest dimension of at least 15 times the depth (where the Repair Data uses this ratio).
   - Blend depth: D minus (A+B)/2.
   - Bow: clearance divided by length, with a default limit of 1/600 that the manual can override.
6. **Fastener and locking reference list**: device type, specification (SP 90, MS 24665, SP 47/55/56, AS 8690 to 8699, AGS 2034 to 2037, SP 134 to 140, SP 41 to 46, SP 107 to 112), re-use rule, wire-lock limits (13 mm, 9.5 mm, 76 mm, 45°), and a split pin size lookup. Use it as task card guidance or a parts-kit rule ("new locking devices required").
7. **Critical bolted joint register**: joint ID, bolt extraction interval (from the Maintenance Schedule), NDT method, findings (fretting, corrosion, cracks), and disposition (replate, discard, reject).
8. **Rigging baseline per aircraft**: dihedral, incidence (by station), engine offset, tailplane, fin, symmetry dimensions, biplane wire lengths, stagger, and sighting rod readings, each with tolerance and date.
   - Store as log book "actual figures".
   - Trigger a re-baseline when a wing or tailplane is changed.
   - Schedule rigging checks periodically and after events.
9. **Fabric condition tracking**
   - Test date, method (portable tester or lab bursting test), dope type (CAB means a lab test is required), lowest reading, percentage of new-fabric strength.
   - Status: Airworthy (at least 70%), Marginal (increase the frequency), Unserviceable (below 70%).
   - Tie the check to Maintenance Schedule intervals and the C of A renewal or ARC airworthiness review.
   - Record the covering and dope scheme (BS X26/751, 752 or 753, fabric spec, envelope or blanket method).
   - Re-cover or re-dope triggers re-weighing and control surface static balance.
10. **Paint and dope material control**: batch receipt date, storage life expiry, pot life for twin-pack materials, shop temperature and humidity log with validation against the ranges (paint 15 to 25°C and below 75% RH, etch primer at least 30% RH, epoxy DTD 5555 above 18°C, fabric covering 16 to 21°C and 70% RH or less, doping 21 to 26°C and 20 to 70% RH), a relative humidity calculator from Table 1, and stage inspector sign-off.
11. **Composite damage finding** object
   - Structure category (Primary, Secondary, zone), damage class (cosmetic or structural, with subtype), detection method (visual, detailed visual, light, tap, NDT type), dimensions, disbond failure mode.
   - Rules:
     - Cohesive disbond creates a wider inspection task.
     - Smooth interface disbond requires a whole-joint repair.
     - Fibre damage on primary structure requires immediate repair.
     - A non-repairable zone requires the manufacturer to be consulted.
   - Previous repair locations should be flagged for special attention.
   - NDT personnel qualification record (GR 23).
12. **Reference documents list**: CAP 747 GR 10, 16 and 23, BCAR A2-5, A6-2, A6-7, A8-10, B2-5, B6-2, B6-7, D4-1, CS 25.607, AC 43.13-1B, DEF STAN 03-2/1, DTD 900, 911C, 369, 5555, 5620, 575A, 56D, BS X26, X28, X31, F1, F8, F34, F35, F47, F114, F100, 192, 3900, FED-STD-191, SAE ARP5089.

## 8. Possibly Outdated

- Airworthiness Notice No. 70 is already marked as transferred to CAP 747 GR No. 16. CAP 747 itself has since been restructured, so whether GR 10, 16 and 23 are still current is uncertain (guess).
- 51-80 says fatigue ADs were removed from CAP 747 and that EASA's MDM.028 is addressing ageing aircraft. After UK exit from EASA, UK-registered aircraft follow UK CAA rules. The EASA working group reference is historical, and the statement that the "CAA and EASA" require compliance may no longer apply as written (guess).
- "Documents recognised or approved by the CAA/EASA" for repair data: for UK aircraft since 2021 this should read UK CAA. EASA approval now matters only via UK acceptance arrangements (guess).
- BCAR chapter references (A2-5, A6-2, A6-7, A8-10, B-sections) come from older BCAR Section A and B structures. BCAR Section A Issue 8 has renumbered or removed many chapters, and B6-7 etc. may no longer exist in the same form (guess, check against BCAR A Issue 8).
- "Notify the nearest CAA area office before work commences": the CAA regional and area office structure has largely been centralised (guess).
- "AMEL Category B" refers to the pre-Part-66 BCAR Section L licence. National licences are now largely replaced by Part-66 or UK Part-66 and Section L for non-Part 21 aircraft.
- "Certificate of fitness for flight" and "renewal of the Certificate of Airworthiness": the national C of A renewal model has largely been replaced by non-expiring C of As with an ARC under Part-M or Part-ML. For non-Part 21 aircraft, the equivalent review process is set in BCAR A (guess).
- References to the "Part 4 series of Leaflets" and "Part 1, Chapter F" for NDT are leftovers from earlier CAAIP structures. The current NDT leaflets are F-xx.
- Legislation in 51-140 and 51-160 (Cellulose Solution Regulations, Petroleum Act, Factories Acts, Factory Inspectorate) is largely superseded by HSWA, COSHH, DSEAR and the HSE (guess). COSHH is mentioned.
- Trichloroethylene as a recommended degreaser is now heavily restricted (REACH/UK REACH authorisation) (guess).
- Chromate primers and inhibitors (strontium and zinc chromate) are subject to REACH restrictions (guess).
- DTD and AGS specifications are legacy British standards. Many are obsolete (guess).
- Page dates are 2011 and 2012, with no later revision in this chunk.
